# GLP-1s are a miracle

*And thinking that using them is dangerous or "cheating" is misguided.*

For some people, consuming an amount of calories in line with their desired body weight is a monumental effort. And there's no finish line, it's the work of a lifetime that can feel like a second job. It's a battle many people attempt and mostly lose. Almost all of them find it harder than other people seem to.

There are many reasons to want a healthy weight, to be slim, to be fit or however you want to express it. Being overweight can represent a huge number of significant health risks. Then, there's the societal stigma where being overweight comes with many opportunity costs in hiring, dating, and general desirability in the thousands of small social transactions of life. 

Now in the last few years, one of the biggest health innovations from this century has emerged: *GLP-1 Drugs*. This class of drugs has dramatically reduced the struggle, making it substantially easier to enter a calorie deficit without the effort consuming your entire life. Side effects? They also protect the heart, kidneys, and liver. Extraordinary!

Aside: Strictly speaking the class of drugs I mean is more than GLP-1s like semaglutide, and also include dual and triple agonists like tirzepatide. But for practicality's sake I'll just say GLP-1 here.

Now this sounds too good to be true. So many of these claimed benefits but also scary risks are flying around in the news, and it's all so new who knows what's going on? Everyone feels some way about it, and it's not always grounded in fact. Which is understandable given the news media's tendency to inflate claims for sensationalism. It's a mechanism built to form alarming and/or unbelievable headlines. Millions of people are using these drugs, and it's only expected to grow, but in almost every single conversation I have with someone about this they express some "fact" or opinion that is verifiably false or misguided and viewed out of context. 

That is the puzzle this essay sets out to address. Many people remain deeply wary of these drugs, wary of the side effects, of some imagined long-term catch, and above all of a heavy social stigma that perceives the drugs as a lazy, cheating shortcut. This stigma runs so deep that users are frequently judged *more harshly* than people who simply stay overweight. I think that reaction is due to two distinct factors, one about a misunderstanding of biology and science and one about our social psychology. If you account for both, the conclusion is that no stigma is deserved. The benefits are enormous, the risks are minimal, and judging someone for using these drugs is completely irrational.

## Part I — The drug: huge benefits with no real catch

Let's begin with what these drugs do and what we know about their benefits. The big benefits are well established and based on excellent evidence: large, randomised, placebo-controlled trials.

*The weight loss benefit is unprecedented for any drug.* Between the major drugs they can result in average losses ranging from 15-30%.[1][2][3] By comparison "lifestyle counselling" delivers around 2-3%, and bariatric surgery around 25-35%.[4] When adjusting for risk, it is by far the most effective way of losing weight today.

Weight loss is not the whole story either. A clinical trial has found that semaglutide cut major cardiovascular events by a relative 20%, falling from 8.0% to 6.5% across the ~18k participants.[5] Meaning that for every 67 people treated with this drug, one person was saved from an event. Only about a third of that benefit was statistically explained by weight loss, which strongly implies the drug protects the heart through mechanisms beyond weight loss itself. The same pattern can be seen in other areas: a 24% reduction in serious kidney events in diabetic kidney disease,[6] reversal of inflammatory fatty-liver disease in 63% of patients,[7] and a halving of obstructive sleep apnea severity in many patients.[8] Several of these findings, like the heart finding, appear only partly attributable to the weight, suggesting direct biological effects rather than a reduction in the normal health risks associated with being overweight.

So, what's the catch?

There is no catch. Biologically speaking at least. This is not a new, untested compound whose long-term consequences are a complete mystery. This class of drugs has been in use for diabetes since 2005, more than 20 years![9] A slow, long-term impact doesn't just show up out of nowhere with data sets of this size. If something was coming, the statistics would already be showing it.

That's not to say there are no side effects at all or any negative sides. But the overwhelming majority turn out to be due to the weight loss itself, not of some misunderstood drug mechanism. When you lose a lot of weight quickly in any way (diet, surgery, drug), roughly 25–40% of the lost mass is usually muscle.[10] This is broadly the same whether the weight comes off through dieting or bariatric surgery at the same total loss.[11] This is purely a lack of protein and/or resistance training, not some destructive property of the drug. Interestingly, measured strength and physical function are generally preserved or even improved. A small increase in risk of gallstones and a dip in bone density are also normal costs of losing weight.[12] Costs that would be paid no matter the mechanism. It is easier to lose weight recklessly fast with the drugs than before, to which I say: Don't.

There are also genuinely drug-specific risks that have been raised. These are the risks not explained by weight loss and seem to be directly linked to using the drug. The raised concerns range from the mild to the genuinely alarming: gastrointestinal upset, pancreatitis, a rare eye condition (NAION), and thyroid tumours. Let's evaluate each of these both in terms of how proven they are and how harmful they are when compared to other risks that we commonly accept.

The gastrointestinal effects are common and definitely well proven, but they are mild, transient, dose-dependent, and rarely dangerous.

Pancreatitis, the historical risk flagged by everyone, was studied and did not even separate from placebo in randomised data; virtually no effect was found.[13] The thyroid warning came from rats, and despite investigation, no demonstrated excess in humans has been found.[14]

NAION is the most serious of the unresolved worries. It is effectively a stroke in your eye, where blood flow to the optic nerve is disrupted and vision is lost, often permanently. The data shows roughly 1.4 extra cases per 10,000 patient-years.[15] But even that has not been shown to be caused by the drug rather than by the vascular risk factors of the older diabetic patients in whom it was found. Compared against ibuprofen, one of the most common pain medications: continuous use carries an excess of roughly 40-60 serious upper-gastrointestinal bleeds per 10,000 patient-years.[16] The accepted, unremarkable painkiller's worst common risk is some thirty to forty times larger, in absolute terms, than the most-feared GLP-1 signal. The combined contraceptive pill, taken by tens of millions of healthy young women, raises the risk of a blood clot (occasionally fatal) by 5-6 per 10,000 patient-years and society regards this as a sensible trade.[17]

Personally, when I look at these numbers, GLP-1 drugs seem fairly meek.

To summarise the risks then: The big-sounding harms are mostly just what normally goes hand-in-hand with weight loss and the drug-specific risks are ultra-rare and not even confirmed. What remains is smaller than what we accept from a bottle of ibuprofen. The benefits, meanwhile, are incredible and proven as solidly as anything can be. There's no reason to believe something is hiding in a bush that will jump out decades from now. These drugs are practically a miracle.

## Part II — The biology: thermodynamics reigns, appetite is a lottery

To really understand how these drugs work to cause weight loss we first need to be clear about the underlying biology (and physics) of weight loss. This is something many people misunderstand.

Weight is decided, absolutely and without exception, by calories in versus calories out (CICO). Every way of changing your weight is, directly or indirectly, a lever on one of those two sides. This is the first place people go wrong, with phrases like "I just have a slow metabolism" or "when I'm stressed my body clings to fat and no deficit makes a difference." If your calorie counting shows a real, sustained deficit and the scale does not move, then it's not the science that's wrong; the calorie counting is wrong. To be clear I'm not making some kind of moral accusation. It's what the measurement studies have consistently found. There is a classic study of people who swore they could not lose weight on under 1,200 calories a day. They were put under careful external tracking, and they were under-reporting their intake by about 47% and over-reporting their exercise by about 51%, with no metabolic abnormality at all.[18] The broader literature finds self-reported eating undershoots reality by anywhere from 10% to 45%, and the gap is near-universal.[19] The "broken metabolism" is, almost always, broken counting.

Aside: This is why I've never been a fan of counting calories, since it's genuinely very hard to do accurately. I don't even want to get started on the new AI scanner apps that claim to give you a calorie count from a picture of your food. You could do the same by just guessing yourself rather than asking an AI to take a guess.

But there is a real phenomenon hiding behind the above misunderstanding of CICO. The body can slow its burn after weight loss in the so-called starvation mode. In typical conditions it amounts to 50–150 calories a day that your body stops burning just by existing. Most of that fades within a few weeks of the weight stabilising, but it does make a difference to how hard it *feels* to lose weight. It is a defensive signal by the body, resisting losing weight. It goes hand in hand with the body also increasing hunger when in a state of weight loss. In an experiment, weight-reduced people were given back the leptin their shrinking fat stores stopped producing, and the metabolic slowdown and the extra hunger both vanished, snapping back to pre-diet levels.[20] The body is trying to stall what it perceives as starvation. It is making a deficit *harder to hold.* So someone who is trying to lose weight is fighting an active battle against their body trying to make them gain it back.

Which brings us to the second misconception: the assumption that it is equally hard for everyone, or that the biological playing field is level. It is not. The relevant variable is less "metabolism" (the calories-out of CICO) and more your innate level of hunger and food-drive (the calories-in of CICO). When calories are effectively unlimited and people eat whatever they want, you see enormous variation in the resulting bodies. This isn't surprising. It is the same kind of spread we see in virtually every biological trait. Some people simply *want* to eat more than others, and they did not choose to.

It can be a tricky thought to wrap your head around. Do you see red in the same way as I do? Does your blue look like my pink? Does your 1000-calorie meal feel like my 600-calorie meal? While the question about colour perception is more a task for philosophers, the question about hunger and satiety is something we can study. And we don't all have the same hunger drive.

The evidence that this drive is largely unchosen is overwhelming. Appetite traits (how full a given meal makes you feel and how strongly food cues pull at you) are 60–88% heritable in twin studies.[21] You inherit your sense of satiety about as much as you inherit your height. From a genetic perspective, at the extreme end, there are single-gene mutations that produce relentless, involuntary hunger from infancy.[22] There is clear proof that hunger is biologically generated, and not some question of willpower.

The cleanest way to feel the force of this is to compare the weight lottery to the one we already accept as a lottery: height. The numbers are strikingly close. BMI is around 75% heritable in adult twins;[23] height is around 80%.[24] Both are biologically set, both are largely unchosen, both are roughly bell-curved in the healthy range. The key difference is that height cannot be influenced by effort, whereas weight can be. You can change your weight by thirty kilograms through behaviour; you cannot change your adult height by a centimetre. But notice exactly *what* is unchosen in each case. With height, it is the outcome. With weight, it is the *drive:* the hunger, the food noise, the baseline pull you have to fight. The hand you are dealt is every bit as involuntary as your skeleton. It is only the playing of the hand that involves effort, and that effort is precisely the thing some people must supply in vast quantities to reach a place that costs others nothing at all.

## Part III — Cheating vs. levelling the playing field

Once you accept that the underlying drive is an unchosen biological lottery, the whole idea of "the easy way out" and "cheating" breaks down. There is nothing to cheat *at*. There is only a disadvantage, and a tool for overcoming it. Telling a disabled person who is using a wheelchair that they are cheating seems a bit absurd, doesn't it?

That is what the drug actually does. It quiets the hunger and the intrusive preoccupation with food (what patients and now researchers call "food noise") that some people carry constantly and others have never experienced. Around 57% of people with obesity report this persistent intrusive food-thought,[25] and after starting semaglutide, the share saying they spend "too much time thinking about food" fell from 63% to 15%, and the share reporting "uncontrollable" food thoughts fell from 53% to 15%.[26] *The drug does not **make** people lose weight*. It hands them something the lucky always had for free: the ability to be *intentional* about eating, choosing what and how much they consume rather than waging a daily war against signals from their own bodies.

The status quo requires extraordinary, sustained mental effort from some people and none from others, to arrive at an identical destination. Why not eliminate the struggle? Is it cheating to want that parity without the struggle?

Some people will object that there is virtue in the effort, and that medicating it away is a kind of moral shirking. In the right context, applied to a genuinely noble goal, perhaps effort can be admirable. But effort is not *inherently* praiseworthy. Completing a marathon with a motorcycle rather than running is cheating. Marathons serve no function other than *for the achievement of having done it.* (Charity is often a reason for running marathons too, but if we could raise money for charity by riding motorcycles through marathons then I'd say go for it.) "Running" a marathon on a motorbike is cheating, because you'd be trying to claim praise for having achieved something you didn't earn.

When someone who lives 42km away takes a taxi to their destination we don't accuse them of cheating. Maybe their friend who lives next door walked, but well, they weren't starting at the same place. Up until recently taxis didn't exist, and we don't see how far people live from the destination. We just see them arrive. Now, we start seeing people arrive in taxis, and we think "Lazy..." Little do we know that they would've had to run a marathon. 

My view is this: Praise people for running a marathon. Don't judge anyone for taking a taxi. People struggle with their weight and some of those who struggle succeed but many fail. Neither group is engaging in the struggle for praise from others though. Those who succeed might get some passing praise for their "discipline," but that is not why anyone tries. They just want to be at the destination. They do it for the benefits (health, mobility, comfort, attractiveness, etc.). Strip away the idea that the struggle is the point, and the struggle is revealed as pure deadweight cost, borne unequally for no good reason.

If we could see everyone's starting points, we would think it slightly mad to insist on the harder route for its own sake, and yet that is precisely the demand made of people who could quiet their hunger with a drug but are told they ought to white-knuckle it instead. A person of four feet who needs something from the top shelf can fetch a step-stool, or they can clamber up onto the countertop every single time. Nobody calls the step-stool cheating. Nobody praises the daily clambering as character-building. 

Some might argue that there is a difference between those who genuinely cannot sustain the effort and those who can but just want an easier ride. Yes, for those who cannot sustain the effort the drug is close to a super-power: it buys them, in a weekly injection, the parity they need to take control of their own eating habits. But the case is just as strong for those who *can* hold the line by willpower. Holding that calorie deficit and maintaining an intake below your natural hunger drive through conscious restraint is not free. The dieting mind pays a measurable, ongoing tax. Active dietary restraint occupies working memory, food cues capture attention whether you want them to or not, and the preoccupation degrades performance on other demanding work happening at the same time.[27] The restraint runs as a background process, and it consumes your brain's resources. To spend a large share of your finite attention and self-control, year after year, policing a hunger that costs other people nothing, is a genuine loss. It's a tax levied on you alone for a biological accident. A drug that lifts that load, freeing all that attention for things that actually matter to you, is not some moral failure. It's a miracle.

## Part IV — The social psychology: irrational but natural

So that settles it then? No one will ever judge someone else for taking a GLP-1 to manage their own weight? Well, remember I said there were two problems. We've only dealt with the biology. The second mistake is about how we judge each other and I think it's the more interesting of the two.

Weight stigma is real, common, and treated as uniquely *deserved* in a way almost no other stigma is. As many as 40% of adults with severe obesity report experiencing it. It's the fourth most common form of discrimination overall, and the third for women.[28] Over the last few decades, discrimination against most stigmatised groups held steady or declined (race, gender, and age discrimination), while reported weight discrimination *rose* by about two-thirds.[29] It is moving in the wrong direction because people believe it is justified.

Why do they believe that? Because weight is considered to be *controllable*. The psychology here is fairly well established. We respond to stigmas we judge controllable with anger and blame, and to those we judge uncontrollable with sympathy and help.[30] Weight gets filed, wrongly, under the first heading. When you persuade people that obesity is genetically and metabolically driven, their hostility softens.[31] What is read as a controllable behaviour is the expression of an uncontrollable drive.

We already recognise plenty of other lotteries as irrational, yet they continue to shape our society. Height, race, and gender all carry comparable wage gaps.[32] Tall men are wildly over-represented as CEOs.[33] Beauty carries a lifetime earnings premium of 10–15%.[34] A lower voice pitch gets paid more.[35] A "white-sounding" name on an otherwise identical CV gets 50% more callbacks, equivalent to eight extra years of experience, purely on the name.[36] One classic example is the halo effect, where attractive people are judged dramatically more competent, yet differ negligibly in actual measured intelligence.[37] The judgement is a myth bolted onto an arbitrary trait. We mostly know all this. We file these biases, correctly, under "unfair" and most people when faced with their own bias on these points will at least try to correct for them. So how and why is weight different?

Because the weight lottery hides one level down. With height or a name, the unchosen variable is right there on the surface, plainly arbitrary. With weight, the unchosen is invisible, and what we see instead is *behaviour*: a person eating. Behaviour looks like choice. But as the underlying mechanisms become better understood by most people, the hope is that taking a GLP-1 comes to be seen the way we see a person with sensitive skin using lots of sunscreen rather than the way we see an athlete injecting steroids.

The strongest challenge this optimistic story faces is that "pharmacological shortcuts" brings an entirely new stigma. In recent studies, people who lost weight on a GLP-1 were judged *more harshly* than people who stayed heavy and did nothing.[38] They were rated lower on warmth, competence, and character, blamed more, and kept at greater social distance. The mechanism is known as "effort-moralisation", where observers assume the medicated person exerted less effort, and equate less effort with less moral worth. Pairing diet and exercise with the medication makes no difference.[39] Losing weight by drug apparently does not buy escape from stigma.

And this new stigma is, if anything, *less* rational than the one it replaces. The original mistake was to blame people for a thing they couldn't control (hunger). The new mistake is to blame them for *doing something about it*. The only thing in this whole picture a person actually controls: not how hungry they feel, but how much they eat. The drug is precisely the act of taking control. To condemn someone for that is to condemn them for an actively chosen, responsible, effective thing available to them. Nobody accuses a statin user of cheating their cholesterol, or a short-sighted person of cheating with glasses. The difference is only that weight loss is visible and has been thoroughly moralised.

There is reason to hope this second-order stigma is transitional. We have watched it dissolve before. Botox, orthodontics, and hair transplants all carried a whiff of vanity and fakery and have substantially normalised as they became common. The drugs can win the battle that is the biological inequality, erasing a disadvantage no one chose. Gradual education and acceptance can dull the social stigma. But neither of these things can win the wider war, which is not about weight at all.

*We judge each other unequally* because we are built to. Ask why almost any human tendency exists and the answer usually falls out of natural selection, and this is no exception. A species that selects mates and leaders by judgement rather than at random prunes its gene pool toward fitness. Similarly, the *dimensions* of judgement are not hard-wired; rather, they shift and change. A species with a fixed criterion would be erased when the environment shifted, whereas one that simply possesses the tendency for judging, and lets the targets drift with circumstance and social agreement, adapts and survives. There have been some experiments that show this, where categories we treat as primal, like race, can be made to fade in the mind when the social cues are rearranged, revealing them as flexible byproducts of a more general machinery for sorting people.[40] Which means the realistic prediction is not that stigma vanishes. There will always be something we judge each other about. Quiet the food noise and erase the trait, and the human urge to rank one another will simply find its next foothold. Solving that would probably take an actual miracle.
